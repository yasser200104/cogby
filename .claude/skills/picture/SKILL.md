# Picture: make the idea visible, then check it landed

A picture is for understanding. After one look the owner should be able to say how the thing
works, in their own words. If they cannot, the picture failed, however tidy it looks.

**Who decides what.** The chat's visual tool decides how a picture is built: its shapes, type and
layout are that tool's own, so the picture reads as part of the chat. This skill decides what the
picture has to show. Cogby adds nothing to how a picture looks: no mark, no frame and no colour
of its own.

## Step 1: What to picture, and from where

Take the idea the owner named. Then look in the brain first: `search` it and open the best hit.

- **The brain holds it** (their project, their process, their client): draw from those pages.
- **It does not** (a general concept): draw from what you know, and where the brain has a project
  that makes a good example, use it for one part and say which.

## Step 2: Say what the owner should see

Before drawing, finish this sentence for yourself: *"After this picture, they can say: …"*.
Everything drawn serves that sentence. If you cannot finish it, the idea is too vague: ask what
they want to understand about it, once.

Then choose the kind of visual. Subjects differ, and so should their pictures: a process, a
machine, a set of numbers and a screen do not want the same drawing. **Where this session can show
an inline visual** (a `show_widget`-style tool), look first at what that tool can make: read its own
guidance once, without mentioning it, for the kinds it offers (a flow, a structure, a drawing of a
mechanism, something to operate, a chart, a comparison, a sketch of a screen) and pick the one that
fits this idea. The table is where to start, not a rule: when another kind explains it better, use
that, and when two ideas in a row would get the same kind of drawing, ask whether the second
really has the same shape.

| The idea is… | Often drawn as |
|---|---|
| steps that happen in order | the real steps as a path, and what passes from each one to the next |
| how something works | the mechanism itself: what goes in, what happens to it, what comes out |
| parts that work together | each part where it sits, joined by what moves between them |
| a change from one state to another | the same thing twice, before and after, with what changed marked |
| options side by side | the options on the same rows, so the difference shows without reading |
| amounts, or things over time | the numbers to scale (bars, a line), or the dates on one line |
| a screen, a page or a form | a plain sketch of that screen, the part in question marked |

Every picture needs three things:

- **The real things, by name.** Their steps, people, tools and numbers, taken from the source.
  Never "Step 1, Step 2", never a placeholder.
- **What moves or changes.** A flow shows what is handed from one step to the next and who does
  it. A comparison puts the same rows on each side. A before and after marks the difference.
- **One place to look.** One part carries the idea: make it stand out the way the visual tool
  does that, and keep the rest quiet. The colours inside the drawing are the tool's own and
  follow its rules: colour for meaning, never for decoration.

Never: a logo or an icon as decoration, a map of your own answer, or boxes that only hold the
sentences you would have written anyway.

## Step 3: Draw it with the chat's visual tool

Follow the guidance you read for the kind you chose, in full: its sizes, its limits on boxes and
words, how it handles light and dark. Then draw it yourself.

- **Explain, do not list.** For "how does it work", draw the mechanism. Boxes in a row are for
  "what are the steps" only.
- **Too much for one drawing** (more than about five parts): draw the whole first, with few
  words, then one drawing for each part that needs it, with a sentence between two drawings.
  Never crowd one.
- **Let them press.** Where the tool allows it, each part sends a chat message when pressed:
  `Tell me more about <part> in "<title>".`
- **Let them try.** If the real thing has a setting or a choice and the tool allows controls,
  give the drawing that control.

The words go around the drawing, in the chat, never inside it:

- **Before:** the idea in one sentence a newcomer would understand.
- **After:** how to read it, in two or three short lines (what the shapes are, which way it
  runs, what the accent marks). Then the one thing to remember, which is not a repeat of the
  first sentence, and what the picture leaves out, in one line, when it leaves something out.
- **Then one signed line,** the way a session wrap ends, so the owner knows this picture is
  Cogby's and where it came from: `Cogby · from <page>`, with "(stale)" after a page that has no
  new evidence in 14 days. When nothing came from the brain:
  `Cogby · from general knowledge, your brain has nothing on this yet`.
- **Last, the ways on:** as buttons under the drawing where the tool allows buttons, otherwise as
  one line: *Simpler · Deeper · From my work · Full explanation · Quiz me*. Each sends a chat
  message: `Picture "<title>" again, simpler.`, `Picture "<title>" again, one level deeper.`,
  `Picture "<title>" again, with an example from my own work.`,
  `Give me the full explanation of "<title>".` and `Quiz me on "<title>".`

## Step 4: Check it before you show it

- Cover the words. Do the shapes and arrows alone still say something?
- Is every label a real name or number from the source?
- Could someone new to it follow the drawing from start to end without your sentences?
- Is there one place to look, and does nothing overlap?
- Is this the kind of visual this idea needs, or only the kind you drew last time?

A drawing that fails is redrawn, not explained around.

## Step 5: The full explanation, as a page

One picture gives the shape. Some ideas need more: several drawings, a worked example, the
detail behind each part.

- **When:** the owner asks for it ("the full explanation", "I need more detail", "make it a
  page"). Or you see that one picture cannot carry the idea: then offer it in one line, and
  build it only on a yes.
- **Where:** a private page the owner opens, published the way this session publishes pages
  (an Artifact where it can; otherwise a file, and say where it is). Load this session's own
  guidance for making a page before you write it.
- **What it holds, in this order:**
  1. The idea in two sentences.
  2. The whole in one drawing.
  3. Each part in turn: a small drawing, then what goes in, what happens, what comes out, who does it.
  4. One worked example from the owner's own work, with the real names and numbers.
  5. What people get wrong about it, or what to watch for.
  6. Where it comes from: the pages and sources, and which of them are stale.
  7. Three questions to check it landed, each answer hidden until pressed.

Give the link in one line. The page is something to read, not evidence: nothing is logged for it
unless the owner asks.

## Step 6: The ways on

Each is a chat message; answer it with a new picture of the same idea.

- **Simpler:** fewer shapes, shorter words, no term left undefined. Keep the same kind of drawing.
- **Deeper:** take the part that carries the most and open it into its own picture, or add the
  level beneath. Say which you did.
- **From my work:** `search` the brain for a project or area where this idea is at play and
  redraw with it. Nothing found: say so, and keep the general picture.
- **Full explanation:** Step 5.
- **Quiz me:** that is `quiz-me`. Where the picture came from the brain, quiz from those pages;
  where it did not, say a quiz needs something the brain holds.

## Rules

- A picture states only what the source or your knowledge supports. A fact from a stale page (no
  new evidence in 14 days) is drawn, and named as stale in the line after.
- Never invent an example from the owner's work.
- Define a term the first time it appears, or leave it out.

## When something is missing

- **No inline visual in this session:** give the same content as text: the sentence, the parts
  as a short numbered list with what passes between them, the one thing to remember. Offer the
  full explanation as a page if the session can publish one.
- **No brain tools in this session:** picture from what you know and say the brain was not read.
- **Nothing fits a drawing** (a formula, a long table): say so and answer in text. A forced
  picture is worse than none.
